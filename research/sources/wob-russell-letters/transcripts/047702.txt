W. O. Brigstocke to Bertrand Russell
4 Hatherley Road, Kew Gardens, 13 May 1919
Autograph letter, two pages. Bertrand Russell Archives, RA1 710, box 5.05.

Words underlined in the manuscript are marked _thus_.

4 Hatherley Road
Kew Gardens. 13. v. 19

Dear Sir

Thank you for your letter. I am disappointed you think I have gone too far: that, of course, is just the point I wanted to know about.

When I read your "Knowledge of the External World" I felt that you hadn't gone far enough: but I could not see why until I read Vaihinger. Vaihinger has been to me what Frege has been to you. Of course, I never meant to say that we can know _nothing_ of natural phenomena: if you read that, I must have expressed myself very badly. I am afraid I am presumptuous, but to put it quite frankly, I feel that Vaihinger has helped me to go a step further than anything in your published books: that is why I asked you to read the part that I meant to explain this first step.

If I understand your position correctly, you say that a table can be seen as an 'aspect': what I see is one 'view' of the table: you admit in fact that I do 'see' a table in the ordinary sense of the word. I cannot persuade myself that we know anything except that there is a 'raw material' out of which we construct our 'things' — a raw material which is & must be always quite unknown to us, if only because we are sensitive to only a part. If in mathematics we can have certainty because we hold the ONE and despise the WHAT: why not in all knowledge? It may be my mathematical ignorance, but I cannot see the _logical_ difference between a name & a number: in mathematics you are content with _five_ without asking for the _what_: why not be content with the name cat without asking for the _what_? And I cannot understand why you should be content with √2 and yet go hunting for a "typical Frenchman". The difficulty — if it is a difficulty — is the same in both.

People like Loeb who are trying to find out what an _egg_ does have the laboratories of the world at their disposal. Nobody seems to think of experiment when it is not what the egg is that is in question. I should like to have a chance of trying to prove experimentally that you can't see the table! I can't believe that it is impossible to devise such experiments.

With very many thanks.
Yours faithfully
W. O. Brigstocke
